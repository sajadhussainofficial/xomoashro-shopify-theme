<p>Himalayan shilajit is a dark mineral resin that seeps from rock high in the Himalaya and Karakoram mountains. It forms over centuries as plant matter trapped in the rock slowly breaks down. "Pure" shilajit should mean two things: nothing has been added to it, and it has been cleaned and tested so it is safe to eat.</p>
<p>This article looks at where Himalayan shilajit comes from, what is in it, how traditional medicine describes it, and how to avoid fakes. For the basics, start with <a href="/blogs/guides/what-is-shilajit">What is shilajit?</a></p>
<h2>Where it comes from</h2>
<p>Shilajit is found in several mountain ranges, but the Himalaya and the Karakoram are the best known. In Pakistan it is collected in the high valleys of Chitral and Gilgit-Baltistan, at altitudes where summers are short and the rock is exposed.</p>
<p>It forms when plants and microbes trapped in cracks in the rock decompose under pressure for a very long time. In summer, warmth softens the material and it seeps out of the rock face as a dark, sticky resin. Collectors climb to these spots and scrape it off by hand, which is slow and often dangerous work.</p>
<h2>Stories and folklore</h2>
<p>Shilajit has gathered many stories. One tells of villagers who noticed monkeys chewing a dark substance from the rocks and decided to try it. Another claims scorpions help release it from the stone. These are folk tales, not science, but they show how long people in the mountains have known about it.</p>
<p>The Sanskrit name is usually translated as "conqueror of mountains", and older texts call it a "destroyer of weakness", which says a lot about how it was valued.</p>
<h2>What is in it</h2>
<p>Shilajit is mostly made of humic substances, the same family of natural compounds found in rich soil. The best known is fulvic acid. It also contains dibenzo-α-pyrones, small amounts of protein, and trace minerals such as iron, zinc and magnesium.</p>
<p>You will often read that shilajit contains "85 minerals". The real number and amount vary with the rock it came from, so treat that figure as marketing rather than a measurement. A lab report on the actual batch tells you far more.</p>
<h2>How Ayurveda describes it</h2>
<p>In Ayurveda, shilajit is a <em>rasayana</em>: a substance taken to support vitality and long life. Classical texts such as the Charaka Samhita describe it at length. In Ayurvedic terms, its taste (<em>rasa</em>) is astringent, pungent and bitter, its potency (<em>virya</em>) is warming, and its after-effect (<em>vipaka</em>) is pungent. It is said to suit the <em>vata</em> and <em>kapha</em> body types.</p>
<p>Traditional texts list many uses for shilajit, including for serious illnesses. These are historical uses, not proof that it works, and shilajit should never replace treatment from a doctor.</p>
<h2>What modern research says</h2>
<p>Scientists have been studying shilajit for decades, but good human trials are still few. A review in <em>Phytotherapy Research</em> found that most studies were done in the laboratory or in animals, pointing to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity and a possible role in reducing tiredness. It called for more well-controlled human studies.</p>
<p>You may read that shilajit can prevent Alzheimer's disease. That claim comes from laboratory research: a 2012 review described experiments in which fulvic acid stopped a brain protein linked to Alzheimer's from clumping together in a test tube. The authors themselves said clinical trials were needed. There is no evidence that shilajit prevents or treats Alzheimer's in people.</p>
<h2>What makes shilajit pure</h2>
<p>Raw shilajit comes off the rock with stone, sand, microbes and sometimes metals. It has to be dissolved, filtered and dried before it is safe to eat. Even then, only a lab test can show what is in it.</p>
<p>Heavy metals are a real concern with traditional products. A study in <em>JAMA</em> bought 193 Ayurvedic medicines online and found that about one in five contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic. That study was not about shilajit specifically, but it is a good reason to ask any seller for a heavy-metal report.</p>
<h2>How to spot fake shilajit</h2>
<p>Simple checks at home can help you avoid the worst fakes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It dissolves fully</strong> in warm water, leaving no grit or oily film.</li>
<li><strong>It changes with temperature:</strong> soft and sticky when warm, hard and brittle when cold.</li>
<li><strong>It smells earthy</strong>, not sweet or like tar or plastic.</li>
<li><strong>The price is realistic.</strong> Real shilajit is slow to collect and clean.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Where does the best Himalayan shilajit come from?</h3>
<p>From high-altitude rock in the Himalaya and Karakoram. In Pakistan, Chitral and Gilgit-Baltistan are the main sources.</p>
<h3>Is Himalayan shilajit different from other shilajit?</h3>
<p>Shilajit from any region is made the same way in nature, but its make-up depends on the local rock and plants. Testing matters more than the region on the label.</p>
<h3>Does shilajit really contain 85 minerals?</h3>
<p>It contains many trace minerals, but the exact number varies from batch to batch. A lab report is the only way to know.</p>
<h3>Can shilajit prevent Alzheimer's disease?</h3>
<p>No. Laboratory studies are interesting, but there is no evidence that it prevents or treats Alzheimer's in people.</p>
