<p>When you shop for shilajit in Pakistan you will see two names: Aftabi and Agni (sometimes Aatshi). They describe how the shilajit was dried after it was cleaned. Aftabi shilajit is dried slowly by the sun; Agni shilajit is dried with heat from a fire.</p>
<p>Tradition prefers Aftabi, and so do most buyers. But the drying method is only one part of quality. This guide explains both methods, the four classical "metal" types you may read about, and what to check before you buy.</p>
<h2>Where the names come from</h2>
<p><strong>Aftabi</strong> comes from <em>aftab</em>, the Persian and Urdu word for the sun. <strong>Aatshi</strong> comes from <em>aatish</em>, fire, and <strong>Agni</strong> is the Sanskrit word for fire. So "Aftabi shilajit" simply means sun-dried shilajit, and "Agni" or "Aatshi" shilajit means fire-dried.</p>
<h2>How shilajit is cleaned</h2>
<p>Raw shilajit is collected from cracks in high mountain rock and is full of stone, sand and plant matter. Whatever the drying method, cleaning starts the same way: the raw material is soaked in water so the shilajit dissolves, then the liquid is filtered to remove the grit. What happens next is the difference.</p>
<h2>Aftabi shilajit: dried by the sun</h2>
<figure><img src="{{file:xomoashro-journal-best-shilajit-breakdown-aftabi.webp}}" alt="Illustration of shilajit drying in shallow dishes under a bright sun in the mountains" width="1024" height="1024"></figure>
<p>In the Aftabi method, the filtered liquid is poured into wide, shallow pans and left in the sun. The water evaporates slowly, over weeks, and the liquid thickens into a soft resin. It takes time, space and good weather, which is one reason Aftabi shilajit costs more.</p>
<p>Ayurvedic tradition regards sun-dried shilajit as the better kind, on the reasoning that gentle drying keeps more of its natural character. That belief is old and widely shared, but no published study has compared Aftabi and Agni shilajit directly.</p>
<h2>Agni or Aatshi shilajit: dried over fire</h2>
<figure><img src="{{file:xomoashro-journal-best-shilajit-breakdown-aatshi-agni.webp}}" alt="Illustration of a clay pot heated over an open fire" width="1024" height="1024"></figure>
<p>In the Agni method, the filtered liquid is heated to drive off the water quickly. It is faster and does not depend on the weather, so it is cheaper to make. Strong heat can change a natural material, which is why tradition rates it below Aftabi. You may see claims that fire-dried shilajit loses a fixed share of its strength; we have not found a study that measures this.</p>
<h2>Which is better?</h2>
<p>If you are choosing between two products that are otherwise equal, sun-dried Aftabi shilajit is the traditional preference, and it is what Xomoashro sells. But a well-made Agni shilajit with a clean lab report is a better buy than an Aftabi shilajit with no testing at all. The label tells you how it was dried; only a lab test tells you whether it is safe.</p>
<h2>The four classical types</h2>
<p>Classical Ayurvedic texts describe four kinds of shilajit, named after the metal they were thought to be linked to, each with its own colour:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Swarna (gold):</strong> described as reddish.</li>
<li><strong>Rajata (silver):</strong> described as pale or whitish.</li>
<li><strong>Tamra (copper):</strong> described as bluish.</li>
<li><strong>Lauha (iron):</strong> blackish-brown. Tradition values it most, and it is the kind almost all shilajit sold today resembles.</li>
</ul>
<p>These names come from tradition, not from modern chemistry. Real shilajit varies with the rock and the plants it formed from, so its make-up changes from one mountain to the next. When you buy, a lab report tells you more than a traditional name.</p>
<h2>What the research says</h2>
<p>Modern research on shilajit is still limited. A review in <em>Phytotherapy Research</em> found relatively few well-controlled human studies, though laboratory and animal work suggests antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity and a possible role in reducing tiredness. None of this research compares drying methods. For more, see <a href="/blogs/guides/what-is-shilajit">What is shilajit?</a></p>
<h2>How to choose good shilajit</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Purified, not raw.</strong> Raw shilajit should never be eaten.</li>
<li><strong>A clear source.</strong> The seller should say which region it comes from.</li>
<li><strong>The drying method.</strong> Aftabi if you follow tradition.</li>
<li><strong>A lab report.</strong> Especially for heavy metals.</li>
<li><strong>Honest claims.</strong> Be wary of sellers who promise cures.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>What does Aftabi shilajit mean?</h3>
<p>Sun-dried shilajit. <em>Aftab</em> means sun in Persian and Urdu.</p>
<h3>Is Aftabi shilajit better than Agni shilajit?</h3>
<p>Tradition says yes, because it is dried gently. No study has compared the two, so testing and source matter more than the name.</p>
<h3>What is Lauha shilajit?</h3>
<p>The blackish-brown "iron" type described in Ayurveda. It is the kind most shilajit sold today resembles.</p>
<h3>Why is Aftabi shilajit more expensive?</h3>
<p>Sun-drying takes weeks and depends on the weather, so less can be made at a time.</p>
